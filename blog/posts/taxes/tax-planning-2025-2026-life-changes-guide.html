Tax Planning for 2025–2026: How to Prepare Your Money Before Life Changes Hit

Updated October 28, 2025 ~14–17 min read

Tax season is usually treated like a one-night stand with the government: you think about it once, get it over with, and try not to think about it again until next year.

But real life doesn’t move in one-year blocks. Raises, new jobs, moving to a different state, getting married, having a kid, starting a side hustle — those things hit whenever they feel like it. If you only react at tax time, you end up surprised, under-withheld, or leaving money on the table.

This guide is about something different: using the window between now and the end of 2026 to plan forward so your taxes line up with the life you’re actually living, not just the life you had last year.

Think of 2025–2026 as a two-year “tax season” where you’re adjusting a little at a time instead of panicking once a year.

Meet Brianna and Malik: same income, different plan

Brianna and Malik both earn around the same amount of money. Both are in their early 30s, both work full-time, both have side projects.

Brianna treats taxes like a surprise quiz. She waits until March, pulls everything together, and hopes for a refund.

Malik treats taxes like a monthly bill he can influence. Every few months, he tweaks his withholding, checks how his side income is tracking, and thinks about next year’s return while this year is still happening.

By the time 2026 rolls around, they’ve each had:

  • A raise
  • A new address
  • A change in side income

Brianna ends up owing more than she expected — twice. Malik ends up right where he aimed: no panic, no surprise bill, no giant refund that means he overpaid.

Step 1: Use 2025 to preview your 2026 tax situation

Your 2025 tax return (filed in 2026) is not just something to get through. It’s a blueprint for what will happen the following year if nothing changes.

After you file, look for four numbers:

  1. Your total income
  2. Your total tax
  3. How much was withheld from your paychecks
  4. Your final result: refund or amount owed

Those four lines tell you if you were overpaying or underpaying during the year — and by roughly how much.

To turn last year’s return into a quick plan, use the 2025–2026 Tax Balance Tuning Calculator and plug in your actual numbers.

Step 2: Planning around a raise or promotion

A raise feels great until you realize your paycheck didn’t go up as much as you expected — and your tax bill might jump the following year.

Instead of guessing, treat a raise like this:

  • As soon as your raise hits: look at one full “new” paycheck.
  • Compare your take-home pay now vs. last year’s paystubs.
  • Decide ahead of time what slice of that extra cash is “untouchable” (for taxes, savings, or debt).

Then, update your W-4 instead of waiting for tax season to yell at you.

Example: Malik’s 2025 raise

Malik’s pay jumped by around $8,000 a year. Instead of letting lifestyle creep eat the whole raise, he did three things in the same week:

  • Increased his 401(k) contribution by 1–2 percentage points
  • Updated his W-4 so a little more tax is withheld
  • Set up an auto-transfer into savings for part of what’s left

By the time he files his 2025 return, that one decision has shaped his 2026 taxes ahead of time.

Step 3: Planning for a move — especially if it’s to a new state

Moving from one city to another inside the same state usually doesn’t change much beyond local tax rules. Moving states can change everything: withholding tables, credits, tax rates, and even whether the state taxes wage income at all.

Before you move (or as soon as you can after), do three things:

  1. Find out whether the new state has income tax and what the basic brackets look like.
  2. Update your address with your employer and any side-hustle platforms.
  3. Adjust your state withholding once you see your first full paycheck in the new state.

Don’t wait until your first tax season in the new state to find out how different it is.

Step 4: Planning for marriage or divorce over 2025–2026

Your relationship status on December 31 is what counts for that year’s return. But the effects can spill across multiple years if your income, dependents, or withholdings change.

If you’re getting married

Talk through these before the year ends:

  • Will you file jointly or separately for that year?
  • How much does each person earn, and how stable is it?
  • Are there student loans, tax debts, or other obligations that might affect a refund?

Once you have rough answers, both people should review their W-4 forms. Many couples get surprised because they never changed the “Single” settings they had when they first started working.

If you’re separating or divorcing

The tax side of separation often shows up a year later, when both people file without fully coordinating:

  • Who’s claiming which dependents?
  • Who’s deducting certain expenses?
  • Who has to adjust withholding because they moved out?

Planning this ahead of time, even at a basic level, can keep 2026’s tax season from becoming Round Two of the same argument.

Step 5: Planning around a new child or new dependent

Having a child or taking in a dependent doesn’t just change your grocery bill and sleep schedule — it changes your filing status, credits, and sometimes your refund or balance due.

For the 2025–2026 window, think about:

  • Who will claim the child (if multiple adults could)
  • How much daycare or childcare you’ll realistically pay
  • Whether one of you will work fewer hours or take leave

As those answers get clearer, you can adjust withholding and start tracking those childcare expenses in one place instead of waiting to reconstruct them next year.

Step 6: Planning for growing side income

Side income is sneaky. It often starts small — a few deliveries, a couple freelance projects, some marketplace sales — and suddenly you realize you made real money but didn’t set anything aside.

Across 2025–2026, the moment your side income stops being an experiment and starts being a pattern, you can:

  • Open a separate bank account just for that income
  • Move a fixed percentage (for example, 25–30%) into a “tax bucket” in that account
  • Track expenses that truly relate to that work

That way, when you sit down to file, you’re not digging through random bank statements. The taxes on that income won’t feel like they’re coming out of thin air.

To decide how much to set aside from side income, use the 2025–2026 Side Income Set-Aside Calculator .

Step 7: Planning around retirement contributions

The contribution limits to retirement accounts (like 401(k)s and IRAs) are one of the few levers you can pull that affect your current tax bill and your future at the same time.

Instead of thinking “I’ll see what’s left in December,” use 2025 and 2026 like this:

  • Pick a contribution target now (for example, 1–2% higher than last year).
  • Increase your contribution at the same time as your next raise.
  • Recheck once mid-year to see if you can bump it slightly again.

Those small adjustments reduce your taxable income in the current year and build a habit that compounds over both years and beyond.

Step 8: Turning tax planning into a simple routine

You don’t need spreadsheets and quarterly projections if that’s not your style. A simple rhythm is enough:

Every quarter (four times a year)

  • Look at your paystubs — how much tax has been withheld so far?
  • Check how much you’ve made from side income.
  • Ask: did anything big change (job, move, relationship, baby)?

Once a year, mid-year

  • Run a quick “if the year ended today” check using your pay and side-income numbers.
  • Adjust your W-4 if you’re clearly trending toward a big bill or a big refund.

That’s it. Not 50 steps. Just a habit of checking in while there’s still time to steer the ship, instead of reacting after it’s already docked.

How Brianna and Malik look by the time 2026 ends

By the close of 2026:

  • Both have had multiple pay changes.
  • Both have had some side income.
  • Both dealt with at least one big life shift.

But:

  • Brianna is still letting tax season surprise her — sometimes with a nice refund, sometimes with a bill she has to put on a card.
  • Malik has turned the 2025–2026 period into a practice run for being “tax aware” all year.

His goal was never to become a tax expert. His goal was to never again feel blindsided in April. And he hit it — by making small decisions in real time instead of waiting for one big moment.

The 2025–2026 mindset shift

If you treat taxes as something that happens to you once a year, they’ll always feel like a surprise. If you treat them as just another part of your money system, they become predictable, even boring.

Over 2025–2026, you can:

  • Use raises to adjust withholding and savings instead of just spending more.
  • Use moves to new jobs or states as chances to reset your tax settings.
  • Plan around family changes instead of scrambling after they happen.
  • Build a simple habit of checking in instead of stressing out.

Then tax season stops being a test of how well you can remember last year, and becomes a report card on decisions you already made on purpose.
