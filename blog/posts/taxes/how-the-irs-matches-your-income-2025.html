How the IRS Matches Your Income in 2025 (Story-Based Guide)

Updated October 22, 2025 ~14 min read

Most people imagine the IRS has a huge team of agents sitting in a room staring at tax returns. But the real system is nothing like that.

In 2025, **98% of income matching is fully automated** — done by a huge system known internally as the Information Returns Processing System (IRP).

This system quietly compares:

  • What YOU reported
  • What your employer reported
  • What apps reported
  • What banks reported
  • What brokerages reported
  • What gig platforms reported

And if anything doesn’t match, you get a notice. Not an audit — just a mismatch letter first.

Translation: The IRS already knows your income before you file.

Meet Layla — the person who didn’t understand IRS matching

Layla works at a hospital, makes $61,000, and bakes custom cakes on the side. She thought the IRS only saw her W-2 and whatever she manually typed.

Wrong.

The payment app she used for her cake business sent the IRS a 1099-K showing $7,842 in payments.

Layla reported only the W-2. The IRS matching system instantly noticed:

“IRS has income: $7,842 Taxpayer reported: $0”

That mismatch triggered the entire process.

How matching actually works (simple version)

Here’s the process in plain English:

  1. Every company that pays you sends the IRS a form with your SSN.
  2. The IRS stores all those forms in a giant database.
  3. When you file, the IRS compares your return to their database.
  4. If any form is missing, late, or mismatched → they send you a letter.

The IRS doesn’t guess — they simply compare numbers

They don’t assume you’re lying. They assume something doesn’t match.

The two most common issues in 2025:

  • Unreported 1099-Ks from apps
  • Gig income mismatches

The IRS matching timeline in 2025

**January–March:** Companies upload income forms.

**April–October:** IRS matches returns filed against those uploads.

**September–December:** IRS sends CP2000 “mismatch notices.”

That means you can get a notice months after filing.

The 14 most common forms the IRS matches in 2025

The IRP system compares your return with:

  • W-2 — job income
  • 1099-NEC — freelance
  • 1099-K — apps/marketplaces
  • 1099-INT — bank interest
  • 1099-DIV — dividends
  • 1099-B — stock sales
  • 1099-R — retirement withdrawals
  • SSA-1099 — Social Security
  • 1099-G — unemployment
  • 1099-SA — HSA withdrawals
  • 1098-T — tuition
  • 1098-E — student loan interest
  • 1098 — mortgage interest
  • 1099-LTC — long-term care

If any of these appear in the IRS system but not on your return — the system flags it instantly.

The “Matching Score” — your invisible IRS grade

The IRS uses an internal score (never shown publicly) to measure how well your return aligns with what they already have.

A return could get:

  • High score — everything matches → no action.
  • Medium score — small mismatch → CP2000 letter.
  • Low score — major mismatch → audit risk.

Case Study: David’s “mismatched W-2” problem

David changed jobs mid-year. One employer mailed the W-2 to his old address. David never got it and didn’t report it.

The IRS had:

W-2: $14,322

David reported:

W-2: $0

That mismatch automatically generated a letter.

Key takeaway: The IRS didn’t “discover” anything — the employer reported it months earlier.

Why mismatches happen even if you didn’t do anything wrong

People assume mismatches mean wrongdoing. But 70% of the time, mismatches happen because of:

  • Companies filing late
  • Companies filing forms with errors
  • IRS receiving duplicate forms
  • Old addresses causing missing forms
  • Name changes after marriage
  • Taxpayer typos
  • 1099-K confusion (apps counted refunds as income)

In 2025, millions of 1099-K forms have mistakes — especially from small platforms.

Yes — even your bank quietly reports to the IRS

Your bank sends a form every year showing:

  • Interest earned
  • Account bonuses

Even $6.38 in interest goes to the IRS automatically.

How gig platforms report your income

Uber, DoorDash, Instacart, and similar companies send:

  • 1099-NEC (cash you earned from the platform)
  • 1099-K (customer-paid amounts, depending on thresholds)

Many gig workers forget one of these, and the IRS catches it instantly.

How payment apps report income

Cash App, Venmo, PayPal, and Zelle all report business transactions.

If you accepted payments for:

  • Services
  • Products
  • Reselling
  • Freelancing

The app files a 1099-K using your SSN or EIN.

How stock platforms report income

If you use Robinhood, Fidelity, E*TRADE, or any brokerage, your 1099-B is sent directly to the IRS.

If you sold stocks for $100,000 and bought for $98,000, the platform reports both numbers.

If you only report one of those numbers, the IRS matching system flags it.

Reminder: The IRS sees your sale proceeds even if you didn’t make a profit.

Crypto platforms and the IRS in 2025

Crypto is fully reported now.

All major platforms send:

  • 1099-DA — Digital Asset sales
  • 1099-B — where applicable

If you sold $1,000 of crypto and rebought instantly, the IRS still receives the data.

The CP2000 letter (most common result of mismatching)

The CP2000 is NOT an audit.

It simply says: “Here’s what we think you earned. Here’s what you reported. Please confirm.”

You can respond by:

  • Agreeing
  • Disagreeing
  • Sending corrected documents

What triggers a REAL audit

Matching mismatches are *not* audits.

But REAL audits often involve:

  • Unreported business income
  • Large itemized deductions that don’t match income
  • Claiming dependents who don’t live with you
  • Unreported investment income

How to avoid mismatches in 2025

1. Make one master folder

Keep all W-2s, 1099s, and documents in one place.

2. Log into apps and platforms

Some companies don’t mail forms — they upload them online only.

3. Use last year’s return as a checklist

Every form you had last year? Expect it again unless something changed.

4. File after February 15

Some companies send corrected forms. Filing before corrections increases mismatch risks.

5. Double-check numbers

A typo can create a $3,000 mismatch.

Miguel worked two part-time jobs and drove for Uber on weekends. He thought filing taxes was guessing — just typing what he remembered.

  • 1099-NEC from Uber

The IRS wasn’t accusing him — they just wanted the numbers corrected.

Ending message: The IRS isn’t guessing. They aren’t chasing you. They’re just matching numbers they already have.
