Tax Filing Basics: A Beginner-Friendly Guide to Doing Your Own Taxes

Updated September 27, 2025 11 min read

If you’ve never filed taxes on your own before, it can feel like walking into a test you didn’t study for. The good news: the process is way more mechanical than mysterious. You collect a few forms, answer questions in order, and then send everything to the government so they can confirm how much tax you actually owe.

This guide is written for people who want a plain-English walkthrough. No complex tax jargon, just what you need to understand the steps and avoid simple mistakes.

Quick reality check: For most employees with one or two jobs and no complicated investments, tax software can handle the heavy math. Your job is mostly gathering information and answering questions honestly.

1. Understand what a tax return actually is

A tax return is just a summary of your year:

  • How much money you earned
  • How much tax was already taken out of your paychecks
  • Which deductions and credits you qualify for

Once the math is done, one of two things is true:

  • You paid too much during the year → you get a refund.
  • You didn’t pay enough → you owe the difference.

Your employer sends the IRS a copy of your wage information. Banks and other companies send copies of your interest, investment income, and more. Your return is your chance to make sure everything is lined up and correct.

2. Collect the documents you’ll need

Most people can get started with a short checklist. Look for these in your mailbox or email around January and February:

Income forms

  • W-2 – from jobs where you were an employee
  • 1099-NEC or 1099-K – from self-employment, freelancing, or gig apps
  • 1099-INT / 1099-DIV – interest or dividends from bank or investment accounts

Other helpful information

  • Social Security numbers for you, your spouse, and any dependents
  • Last year’s tax return (handy for reference)
  • Records of major life events: marriage, new baby, college tuition, etc.

3. Choose how you want to file

There are three main ways to file your taxes. The best choice depends on how comfortable you are and how complicated your finances are.

A. Use online tax software (most common)

Companies like TurboTax, H&R Block, and others offer step-by-step software that asks simple questions and fills in the official forms for you. For many people with W-2 income and standard situations, this is enough.

Pros:

  • Guided questions in plain language
  • Error checks and reminders for missing forms
  • Can e-file (submit electronically) and get refunds faster

B. File directly with the IRS if you qualify

In some years, the IRS offers free direct filing options for certain income levels and simple returns. These tools are designed for people with W-2 income and basic situations. They don’t cover every scenario, but they can be enough if your situation is straightforward.

C. Use a professional tax preparer

If you have a small business, rental properties, complex investments, or you just don’t want to worry about it, hiring a tax pro can be worth it. They’ll ask questions, review your documents, and file on your behalf.

Tip: Even if you use a professional, read through your return before you sign. You’re still responsible for what’s on it.

4. Walk through the sections in order

When you open tax software, you’ll usually see the process broken into chunks. A common flow looks like this:

  1. Personal info – names, addresses, filing status, dependents.
  2. Income – W-2s, 1099s, interest, unemployment, etc.
  3. Deductions – standard deduction vs. itemized (like mortgage interest or large medical bills).
  4. Credits – things like education credits or child-related credits if you qualify.
  5. Review & file – final check, compare refund vs. amount owed, confirm bank info.

Just move through the screens one at a time. If a question doesn’t apply to you, most software gives you a clear “no” or “skip” option.

5. Understand the difference between deductions and credits

This is where a lot of people get confused, so here’s a simple way to think about it:

  • Deductions lower the amount of income that gets taxed.
  • Credits directly reduce the tax you owe, dollar for dollar.

Most people take the standard deduction, which is a flat amount set by law and adjusted most years. You can choose to itemize instead if you have large deductible expenses, but if you don’t recognize that word, standard deduction is often where you’ll land.

Credits are more targeted and based on your situation. For example, there are credits for some education expenses and credits related to children or low-to-moderate income work. Your software will ask the right questions to see what you might qualify for.

6. Decide how to receive your refund or pay a balance

When all the math is done, your return shows either:

  • A refund (you paid in more than needed), or
  • A balance due (you still owe some tax).

If you’re getting a refund

Choosing direct deposit is usually the fastest way to receive it. You’ll need your bank’s routing number and your account number. You can also apply some or all of your refund to next year’s taxes if you want a head start.

If you owe money

There are several ways to pay: direct debit from your bank, debit card, credit card, or mailing a check. If you can’t pay everything at once, the IRS may allow payment plans. Your tax software or preparer will explain the options.

7. E-file and save a copy of your return

Electronic filing is now the standard. It’s faster and typically more secure than mailing a paper return. After you submit, you’ll usually get:

  • A confirmation that your return was accepted
  • A PDF copy you can download and store

Save that PDF in a secure folder or cloud storage. You may need it if you apply for a mortgage, student aid, or anything that uses your income history.

8. When to bring in extra help

While many people can file on their own, it’s smart to get professional advice if you:

  • Start a business or side hustle with real profits
  • Buy or sell property
  • Trade a lot of stocks or crypto
  • Receive letters from the IRS you don’t understand

Think of a tax professional as a coach. A one-time strategy meeting can sometimes save you more than it costs, especially as your finances grow more complex.

9. Make next year easier on yourself

Once you’ve filed your first return, you’ve done the hardest part—breaking the mental barrier. From here, you can make your life easier by:

  • Creating a simple “tax folder” (physical or digital) where you drop documents all year
  • Checking your paystub a few times a year to see how much tax is being withheld
  • Saving a small slice of side-hustle income in a separate account for future taxes

The goal isn’t to become a tax pro. It’s to be organized enough that tax season feels like a routine task, not a crisis.

Once you know the flow—gather, answer, review, file—doing your own taxes becomes a normal part of your money routine rather than something you dread each spring.
