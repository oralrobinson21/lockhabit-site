IRS Audit Red Flags in 2025: What Really Triggers an Audit (Story + Guide)

Updated October 22, 2025 ~14 min read

Nobody wakes up hoping to get a letter from the IRS. And when you do get one — even if it’s a tiny notice — your stomach drops first and your brain catches up later.

But here’s the truth: IRS audits aren’t random. They’re triggered by specific math, mismatches, patterns, and forms the IRS already has. If your return lines up cleanly, you’re usually in the clear.

This guide shows you the exact red flags IRS systems use in 2025 — explained through real stories and simple examples, no tax jargon.

Meet Jordan — the guy who forgot one small form

Jordan isn’t rich or shady. He works two W-2 jobs, does Uber Eats on weekends, and sells sneakers online. In 2024, he forgot to report a $1,138 1099-K from an online marketplace.

He didn’t get audited because he “did something wrong.”

He got audited because the IRS already had a copy of the form — and Jordan didn’t report it.

That mismatch alone triggered a notice. That’s how most audits start.

Red Flag #1: The IRS has income forms you don’t report

This is the biggest trigger of all. The IRS gets copies of:

  • W-2s (job income)
  • 1099-NEC (freelance/gig work)
  • 1099-K (online platforms / payment apps)
  • 1099-INT (bank interest)
  • 1099-DIV (dividends)
  • Brokerage statements (stock trades)

If they have it and you don’t include it, their system flags your return automatically. No human even looks at it yet; the computer catches the mismatch.

In 2025, far more apps and marketplaces send 1099-K forms — even for small sales. Missing these forms is now the #1 cause of IRS letters.

Red Flag #2: Extremely high itemized deductions compared to your income

If someone reports $38,000 of income and claims $29,000 of itemized deductions, the IRS system raises an eyebrow.

It’s not illegal — just uncommon — and uncommon gets reviewed.

Red Flag #3: Claiming dependents you shouldn’t be claiming

This is one of the most common audit triggers, especially after divorces or custody changes.

The IRS often checks:

  • School records
  • Doctor or medical records
  • Daycare contracts
  • Lease or utility bills showing the child’s address

If two people claim the same child — the IRS jumps in immediately.

Red Flag #4: Gig workers with suspiciously low or high expenses

Self-employed people get audited more only because they have more variables.

Examples IRS systems flag:

  • Uber driver reports $19,000 income but only $400 expenses (too low)
  • Photographer reports $12,000 income but $17,000 expenses (too high)

Both extremes trigger reviews.

Red Flag #5: Round numbers everywhere

If your tax return looks too “clean,” it actually looks suspicious.

Real life is messy. Numbers like $2,000, $3,000, $5,000, repeated everywhere, can look like guesses instead of actual records.

Red Flag #6: Large charitable donations on a small income

You can be generous — but if you earn $35,000 and claim $16,000 in charitable gifts, the IRS may want receipts.

Red Flag #7: Huge swings compared to last year

The IRS compares your new return to your old ones. Big changes aren’t wrong, but they often trigger a quick review.

Examples that raise questions:

  • Income jumps from $38k → $71k
  • Sudden new deductions
  • Going from 1 dependent → 3 dependents
  • Side hustle explodes from $1k → $15k

Story: Melissa’s “promotion audit”

Melissa became a store manager and her income jumped by over $30,000 in a year. She didn’t update her W-4, her deductions grew, and the system noticed.

Her audit was simple — everything matched — but the big year-to-year swing triggered it.

Red Flag #8: A business that always loses money

The IRS can reclassify a “business” as a hobby if it never makes profit.

Common hobby-risk areas:

  • Dog breeding
  • Photography
  • Craft shops
  • Reselling

If expenses exceed income multiple years in a row — you may get flagged.

Red Flag #9: Crypto activity that isn't reported

Crypto exchanges now send reports directly to the IRS. So if you buy/sell/swap crypto but leave the crypto section blank on your return — the IRS system flags it automatically.

Red Flag #10: High-income taxpayers (more moving parts)

High-income people don’t get audited because they’re “targeted.” They get audited because:

  • They have rental properties
  • They sell stocks
  • They own businesses
  • They claim complex credits

More complexity = more opportunities for mismatches.

What the IRS almost never audits

  • Simple W-2 returns
  • Standard deduction returns
  • Cleanly matched returns (all forms included)
  • Low-income returns without special credits

If everything matches what the IRS already has — the chance of audit is extremely low.

How to make your return “audit-resistant”

1. Match every IRS form

If you get a form — the IRS already got a copy.

2. Don’t estimate numbers

Good records = clean return.

3. Keep receipts for anything unusual

Especially big deductions.

4. Double-check dependents

Most disputes come from duplicate dependent claims.

5. Track side-hustle income correctly

This is where most mismatches happen.

Story: Tony’s “almost audit” saved by one screenshot

Tony sold his PS5 for $300 on Facebook Marketplace. He still received a 1099-K. If he didn’t report it, the IRS would’ve flagged him.

But he included it properly — as a personal item sold at a loss — and saved the receipt.

That one screenshot avoided a letter.

The bottom line

Audits in 2025 aren’t about luck — they’re about mismatches, math, and patterns. If your return lines up with what the IRS already has, your odds of being audited drop dramatically.
